Was Jerome Powell a Good Fed Chair?

When I started digging into Jerome Powell’s tenure as Chair of the Federal Reserve, I wasn’t looking for a hot take. I wanted to understand whether the outcomes we saw were the result of skill, luck, institutional inertia, or some mix of all three. Powell’s time at the Fed spans one of the most volatile economic periods in modern history. That alone makes any clean verdict difficult. Still, after working through the record, I think it is fair and necessary to ask the central question: Was he a good chairman?

The Unusual Starting Point

One thing that stands out immediately about Jerome Powell is how unorthodox his background is compared to prior Fed chairs. He is not an academic economist. He came from law, investment banking, and private equity. That mattered more than many people initially realized.

Powell’s decision-making style has consistently reflected that background. He tends to focus on financial system plumbing, market functioning, and risk management rather than elegant economic theory. In practice, this made him more pragmatic and less dogmatic than some of his predecessors. Whether that is a strength or a weakness depends on the moment. During crises, it was almost certainly a strength.

Crisis Leadership Was His Strongest Suit

Powell’s legacy cannot be discussed without the COVID crisis. In March 2020, financial markets were not just volatile; they were close to breaking. Treasury markets were seizing up. Credit markets froze. The Fed under Powell acted with speed and scale that would have been unthinkable a decade earlier.

The Fed backstopped corporate credit, stabilized money markets, and signaled unlimited support where needed. From a market stability perspective, the response worked. Liquidity returned quickly and systemic collapse was avoided. From my research, this is where Powell deserves real credit. He understood that hesitation would be more damaging than overreaction. He also showed a willingness to stretch the Fed’s tools to their practical limits while maintaining institutional credibility. That balance is hard to pull off.

The Inflation Problem Cannot Be Ignored

If crisis management was Powell’s high point, inflation was the defining challenge of his second act. The Fed was late to pivot. The initial insistence that inflation was "transitory" aged poorly as price pressures broadened and became entrenched.

This is where Powell deserves criticism. The Fed underestimated how fiscal stimulus, supply chain disruption, and labor market tightness would interact. By the time rate hikes began in earnest, inflation expectations were already drifting upward. That said, once the mistake was clear, Powell shifted aggressively. The pace of tightening from 2022 onward was sharp, deliberate, and politically unpopular. He absorbed public criticism and market volatility without wavering. That willingness to reverse course and own the problem matters in evaluating his overall performance.

Independence Under Pressure

Another underappreciated part of Powell’s tenure is how he handled political pressure. He faced open criticism from a sitting president, pressure from markets, and constant media scrutiny. Throughout it all, he consistently defended the independence of the Federal Reserve.

From a governance perspective, that may be one of his most important contributions. Central bank credibility is not just about inflation numbers; it is about whether the public believes decisions are made based on mandate rather than politics. Powell largely preserved that trust.

So Was He a Good Chairman?

My conclusion is nuanced. Jerome Powell was not flawless. The inflation miss was real and costly. But judged across the full arc of his tenure, I think he was a good Fed chair, and arguably the right chair for an unusually unstable era.

He excelled at crisis management, protected institutional independence, and showed adaptability when conditions changed. His background made him less theoretical but more operationally effective when it mattered most. History will likely remember Powell not as a visionary economist, but as a steady hand during chaos. Given the environment he inherited and the shocks he faced, that may be a stronger legacy than it sounds.
